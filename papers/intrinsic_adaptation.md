# Intrinsic Adaptation: Do Woven Living Muscle Tissues Naturally Hypertrophy Under Load?

## Yoshimitsu Katayama (TheYKHC Tower)

### Abstract

The fourth route to polygon transcendence proposed weaving Composite Living Fibers (CLFs) and exploiting myoblast membrane fusion to create scaffold-free continuous muscle tissue. A critical question follows: does this woven tissue behave like natural muscle? Specifically, does it hypertrophy (grow stronger) under mechanical load? We argue that the answer is yes—necessarily and unavoidably—because the fusion mechanism that builds the tissue (Myomaker/Myomerger) is the same mechanism that drives adaptive hypertrophy in vivo. Satellite cells, the muscle stem cells responsible for post-natal muscle growth and repair, use the identical fusion machinery. A woven CLF construct that has undergone myoblast fusion is, by definition, living muscle fibers. Living muscle fibers respond to load by activating satellite cells, which proliferate, migrate to damaged regions, and fuse into existing fibers—making them thicker and stronger. This process is autonomous: no external designer programs the adaptation. The cells possess the capability (N) intrinsically. The designer's role is limited to D-reduction: removing the scaffold, eliminating interfaces, and creating the space for cells to express their inherent N. This principle—design as liberation rather than imposition—connects the fourth route to the broader V=N/D framework and has profound implications for the longevity and functional integration of woven tissue implants.

### 1. The Question

The fourth route paper established that myoblasts woven into 3D CLF constructs can fuse via Myomaker/Myomerger to form continuous, scaffold-free muscle tissue [Katayama, DOI: 10.5281/zenodo.21799963]. The construct is living muscle at the moment of fusion.

A natural question follows: **Is the woven tissue "finished" at the moment of fusion, or does it continue to develop?**

In the body, muscle is never finished. It constantly remodels in response to mechanical load:
- Weight training increases muscle cross-sectional area
- Endurance training increases mitochondrial density and capillary networks
- Disuse causes atrophy
- Injury activates repair through satellite cell fusion

If woven muscle tissue is truly living muscle, it must exhibit the same adaptive plasticity. If it does not, it is not truly living—it is merely a shaped mass of cells.

We argue that woven muscle tissue **necessarily** exhibits adaptive hypertrophy, because the mechanism that builds it is the mechanism that adapts it.

### 2. The Mechanism: One Machinery, Two Contexts

#### 2.1 Myoblast Fusion During Development

During muscle development, myoblasts (muscle precursor cells) proliferate, align, and fuse to form multinucleated myotubes. Each myotube contains many nuclei within a single shared cytoplasm, enclosed by one continuous membrane. This process is mediated by:

- **Myomaker (TMEM8C)**: Required for hemifusion (outer leaflet merger)
- **Myomerger (Myomixer/MEMI)**: Required for pore formation (full membrane fusion)

Myomaker and Myomerger are the only proteins known to be both necessary and sufficient for myoblast fusion [PMC4508005, PMC7849503].

#### 2.2 Satellite Cell Fusion During Adaptation

In adult muscle, growth and repair are driven by **satellite cells**—quiescent muscle stem cells located between the basal lamina and the sarcolemma of mature muscle fibers.

Upon mechanical load or injury:
1. Satellite cells activate and re-enter the cell cycle
2. They proliferate as myoblasts
3. Some return to quiescence (self-renewal); others commit to differentiation
4. Differentiating myoblasts express **Myomaker and Myomerger**
5. They fuse into existing muscle fibers, donating their nuclei
6. The fiber increases in cross-sectional area (hypertrophy)

**The fusion machinery is identical.** The same Myomaker/Myomerger that built the muscle during development drives the adaptive response in adult muscle.

### 3. Why Woven Tissue Must Adapt

The woven CLF construct undergoes fusion in step 3 of the fourth route process:
1. Weave CLFs into 3D architecture
2. Cells align along weave direction
3. Myoblasts fuse via Myomaker/Myomerger → continuous syncytium
4. Interface dissolved
5. Tissue matures

At step 3, the construct becomes living muscle fiber. At step 5, the muscle matures but the story does not end there.

**If mechanical load is applied to the woven tissue:**

1. The contractile proteins (actin/myosin) generate tension along the weave direction
2. Mechanical stress causes micro-damage in the myofibers
3. If satellite cells are present (either residual from the CLF fabrication or introduced), they activate
4. Activated satellite cells proliferate, differentiate, and express Myomaker/Myomerger
5. They fuse into existing fibers → hypertrophy

**The woven tissue adapts because it is alive, and living muscle adapts because that is what living muscle does.**

The key precondition is the presence of satellite cells in the construct. This can be achieved by:
- Including satellite cells in the CLF hydrogel sheath alongside myoblasts
- Co-culturing the woven construct with satellite cells post-weaving
- The natural presence of satellite cells in primary muscle cell isolates (standard in tissue engineering)

### 4. Design as Liberation: The V=N/D Framework

This finding has a profound implication for the philosophy of tissue engineering.

#### 4.1 Traditional Approach: Design as Imposition

Conventional tissue engineering designs everything:
- Scaffold geometry (pore size, fiber diameter, mechanical properties)
- Growth factor gradients
- Cell seeding density
- Degradation kinetics

The scaffold is the design. The cells are passive occupants. When the scaffold degrades, the tissue must maintain the shape that was imposed. If the tissue fails to maintain the imposed shape, the design fails.

In V=N/D terms: the designer tries to supply N (the capability) through the scaffold. The scaffold IS the N. D (the constraint) is the scaffold's own limitations (stiffness, degradation, foreign body response). V = N_scaffold / D_scaffold.

#### 4.2 Fourth Route Approach: Design as Liberation

The fourth route removes the scaffold entirely. The cells are both fiber and matrix. After fusion, no foreign material remains.

What does the designer provide?
- **Architecture**: The weave pattern (spatial organization)
- **D-reduction**: Elimination of interfaces, removal of foreign scaffold

What does the designer NOT provide?
- Contractility (intrinsic to cardiomyocytes)
- Hypertrophy (intrinsic to satellite cell biology)
- Repair (intrinsic to the muscle's regenerative machinery)
- Adaptation (intrinsic to living muscle's response to load)

In V=N/D terms:
- **N is intrinsic to the cells.** The capability to contract, grow, repair, and adapt is already there—encoded in the genome, expressed through Myomaker/Myomerger and the satellite cell system.
- **D is what the designer removes.** The scaffold (foreign material), the interfaces (layer boundaries), the constraints (fixed geometry that prevents remodeling).
- **V = N_intrinsic / D_designer**

As D_designer → 0 (scaffold removed, interfaces eliminated), V diverges. The tissue's value is not limited by what the designer built, but by what the designer **did not prevent**.

#### 4.3 The Implication

A scaffold-free woven muscle tissue implant does not merely replace lost tissue. It becomes part of the body's living system:
- It contracts with the surrounding muscle
- It hypertrophies when exercised
- It atrophies when unused
- It repairs itself when damaged
- It vascularizes through natural angiogenesis
- It innervates through natural axon sprouting

The implant is not a prosthetic. It is **the body's own tissue, grown in a designed architecture, then liberated to become fully alive.**

### 5. The Cascade of Capabilities

This leads to a cascade of intrinsic capabilities that require no external design:

| Capability | Mechanism | Designer's Role |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| Fusion | Myomaker/Myomerger | Provide initial cell proximity (weaving) |
| Contraction | Actin-myosin sliding | None (intrinsic to muscle cells) |
| Hypertrophy | Satellite cell fusion under load | None (intrinsic to muscle biology) |
| Repair | Satellite cell activation after injury | None (intrinsic) |
| Atrophy | Protein degradation under disuse | None (intrinsic) |
| Vascularization | Endothelial cell migration and tube formation | None (intrinsic, can be enhanced by co-weaving) |
| Innervation | Axon sprouting and NMJ formation | None (intrinsic, can be enhanced by co-weaving) |

The only step that requires design is the initial weaving—the architecture. Everything else is the cells' own capability, liberated by the absence of constraint.

### 6. Implications for Cardiac Muscle

For cardiac muscle specifically, the adaptation question is even more significant:

Cardiomyocytes have limited regenerative capacity in adults. However:
- Cardiac satellite cells / progenitor cells have been identified [various sources]
- The neonatal heart can regenerate after injury (first 7 days in mice)
- Mechanical load shapes cardiac hypertrophy (physiological vs. pathological)

A woven cardiomyocyte CLF construct that fuses into a continuous syncytium:
1. Beats spontaneously (cardiomyocytes are auto-rhythmic)
2. Responds to afterload by remodeling (hypertrophic response)
3. Aligns contraction along the weave direction
4. If woven in the heart's spiral pattern, contracts in the correct helical direction

The question of whether this tissue adapts is not speculative—it is **necessary**, because the alternative (a living muscle that does not respond to load) contradicts the definition of living muscle.

### 7. Experimental Verification

While the theoretical argument is strong (the mechanism is the same), experimental verification would solidify the fourth route:

**Proposed experiment:**
1. Weave myoblast + satellite cell CLFs into 3D construct
2. Allow fusion (Myomaker/Myomerger-mediated)
3. Apply cyclic mechanical stretch (mimicking exercise load)
4. Measure: fiber diameter, nuclei count per fiber, Myomaker/Myomerger expression, force generation
5. Compare with: static culture (no load) and 2D monolayer (no weaving)

**Expected result:** Cyclic stretch → satellite cell activation → fusion into existing fibers → increased fiber diameter and force generation. The woven tissue hypertrophies.

This experiment would demonstrate that the fourth route produces not just structurally correct tissue, but **functionally adaptive living tissue** — tissue that grows stronger with use, just as natural muscle does.

### 9. Conclusion

Woven living muscle tissue, created through the fourth route, does not merely survive—it adapts. The same Myomaker/Myomerger machinery that builds the tissue during fabrication drives its growth and repair throughout its lifetime. Satellite cells, present in the construct or introduced post-weaving, provide the regenerative reservoir.

This means the designer's role is not to build a tissue that works, but to create the conditions under which tissue builds itself. The weaving provides architecture. The fusion provides continuity. The adaptation provides life.

In V=N/D terms: N is the cell's intrinsic capability. D is what the designer removes. As the scaffold dissolves and the interfaces fuse, D approaches zero—and the tissue's value diverges, not because of what was built, but because of what was liberated.

The woven muscle is not designed to grow stronger. It is designed to be free to grow stronger on its own. That is the difference between a prosthetic and a life.
